Interpretation

Teaching points and limitations

Light entering either eye supplies afferent input through the retina and optic nerve. Pretectal pathways activate both Edinger–Westphal nuclei. Parasympathetic efferent signals pass through CN III, the ciliary ganglion, and short ciliary nerves to the iris sphincter.

Direct response means constriction of the illuminated eye. Consensual response means constriction of the opposite eye. A complete afferent defect reduces the response of both pupils when the affected eye is illuminated. A complete unilateral efferent defect prevents that pupil from constricting regardless of which eye is illuminated.

RAPD is a relative afferent finding. During the swinging flashlight test, moving the light from the stronger afferent eye to the weaker eye produces less bilateral constrictor drive. With intact efferent pathways, both pupils may dilate relative to their preceding constricted state. They are not necessarily larger than in darkness.

An isolated afferent defect does not itself create resting anisocoria in this model. Efferent loss is illustrated with a larger resting pupil, but actual pupil size depends on the cause and other autonomic influences.

The model uses equal illumination, a simplified bilateral signal, and illustrative response dynamics. It does not simulate accommodation, light-near dissociation, Horner syndrome, an Adie pupil, pharmacologic effects, or a quantitative clinical RAPD assessment. Efferent settings affect the pupil only, not extraocular movements.
